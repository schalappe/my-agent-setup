# Situation questions

Every question puts the learner in a **situation** and asks what they do. The test of a question: the right answer depends on the facts and constraints of this situation. If someone could pick it without reading the situation, from the options alone, rewrite it.

## The situation

- 3 to 5 lines: who, where, what just happened, which constraint forces the choice. Concrete names, numbers, stakes, drawn from the learner's mission (a tech lead aspirant gets team and codebase situations).
- Invent a fresh instance at every review. Vary the people, numbers, and setting, and also the **deciding constraint**: alternate cases where the rule applies with near cases where one changed constraint makes another action right. A good near case is one where the source's usual practice does not fit (she dislikes perfume) and the principle calls for another way. The learner keeps the judgement, not the answer letter.
- End on a decision: "What do you do first?", "Which option do you pick?", "What do you tell her?".
- For a new competency, stay in the **shallow end**: the situation is solvable from what the learner already knows plus one new element.
- One competency per question. Mixing two competencies muddies the grade.

## The options

- Four options, each an action someone could plausibly take.
- The best option applies the `Rule` in the terms of this situation. Never lift the source's distinctive wording or ritual phrase into it: a phrase only the source would use marks the answer, and trains the learner to recite a practice instead of choosing by the principle.
- Exactly one is best for this situation. When two are defensible, change the situation until one wins, and say in the feedback what would make another option right.
- Draw the wrong options from the competency's `Pitfalls` first, then from mistakes practitioners really make. Each wrong option should tempt someone holding a specific misconception.
- Give all four options the same grammatical shape and the same length, within a few words, so formatting, order, and hedging words give nothing away.
- Rotate the position of the best option: across a session, each position holds it at least once when the session has four questions or more.

## The feedback

- The verdict first, in one line.
- Then each option in turn: why it fits or fails _this_ situation. Name the misconception behind each wrong option.
- Cite the source behind the rule (`S3 p. 12`).
- Keep the tone factual: a wrong answer is information about what to practise next.
